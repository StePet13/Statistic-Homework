4. Theoretical Discussion & Comparative Analysis Homework Requirements

A. Statistical Properties & Length Constraints

The Caesar cipher preserves the underlying frequency distribution of the plaintext, merely shifting the histogram. The $\chi^2$ attack minimizes the distance between observed and expected frequencies. However, as demonstrated in our empirical test:

  • Law of Large Numbers: For very short texts ($L < 30$), the sample variance is too high. The empirical distribution diverges significantly from the expected linguistic probability mass function (PMF), causing $\chi^2$ to fail.
  • Threshold: The attack becomes highly reliable only when $L \ge 50-100$ characters, ensuring common letters (E, T, A) dominate the statistical noise.

B. Comparing Alternative Attacks (Caesar)

  • Exhaustive Key Search (Brute Force): Trivial for Caesar ($\vert{}\mathcal{K}\vert{} = 26$). Requires $O(26)$ operations and a basic language oracle (dictionary). Superior to statistical analysis for short texts.
  • Letter-Frequency Analysis ($\chi^2$ unigram): $O(26 \cdot L)$ operations. No dictionary needed, but requires $L \ge 50$.
  • N-Gram Scoring: Computes the log-likelihood of bigrams/trigrams. More robust than unigrams for short texts because it captures structural syntax, but requires an $N$-gram training corpus.

C. ECDLP Vulnerabilities in the Toy Scheme

While the Brute-Force attack implemented above successfully recovers the key on the toy curve, the toy scheme possesses a far more critical structural flaw:

  • Smooth Order & Pohlig-Hellman: The toy curve $y^2 \equiv x^3 + 7 \pmod{17}$ has a subgroup order $N = 9$. Since $9 = 3^2$ is a smooth number, the Pohlig-Hellman algorithm can solve the ECDLP in $O(\sqrt{3}) \approx 2$ operations. Real curves like secp256k1 use prime orders ($N \approx 2^{256}$) to thwart this decomposition, forcing generic attacks like Pollard's $\rho$ which require $O(\sqrt{N}) \approx 2^{128}$ operations.

D. Scheme Design Weaknesses (Malleability)

  • Simplified KDF Flaw: Our implementation derives the symmetric mask via $\text{SHA-256}(S_x)$. For plaintexts $>32$ bytes, this mask repeats, leading to a "two-time pad" vulnerability. Real ECIES uses HKDF with a counter block.
  • Lack of Authenticated Encryption (AEAD): The scheme applies a raw XOR stream cipher ($C_2 = m \oplus \text{KDF}$). This is highly malleable. An active attacker can flip bits in $C_2$ directly altering the decrypted plaintext without detection. An HMAC or Poly1305 tag is mandatory in production.
