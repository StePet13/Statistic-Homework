1. Mathematical Formulation — Marginal, Conditional & Expected Frequencies Bivariate Categorical Framework $(X, F)$

Joint, Marginal & Conditional Distributions

Given $N$ bivariate observations $\{(x_k, f_k)\}_{k=1}^N$ with $X \in \{x_1, \dots, x_r\}$ and $F \in \{f_1, \dots, f_c\}$, the programmatic contingency table counts joint occurrences $n_{ij} = \sum_{k=1}^N \mathbb{I}(X_k = x_i, F_k = f_j)$:

$$p_{ij} = \frac{n_{ij}}{N}, \qquad p_{i\bullet} = \sum_{j=1}^c p_{ij} = \frac{n_{i\bullet}}{N}, \qquad p_{\bullet j} = \sum_{i=1}^r p_{ij} = \frac{n_{\bullet j}}{N}$$

The conditional frequency distributions (row and column profiles) are:

$$P(F = f_j \mid X = x_i) = \frac{n_{ij}}{n_{i\bullet}} = \frac{p_{ij}}{p_{i\bullet}}, \qquad P(X = x_i \mid F = f_j) = \frac{n_{ij}}{n_{\bullet j}} = \frac{p_{ij}}{p_{\bullet j}}$$

Independence Hypothesis ($H_0$), Expected Counts & Pearson's $\chi^2$

Under statistical independence ($H_0: X \perp\!\!\!\perp F$), joint probabilities factorize into the product of marginals ($p_{ij} = p_{i\bullet}p_{\bullet j}$), yielding the expected frequencies:

$$E_{ij} = N \cdot \hat{p}_{i\bullet} \cdot \hat{p}_{\bullet j} = \frac{n_{i\bullet} \cdot n_{\bullet j}}{N}$$

Discrepancies between observed $n_{ij}$ and expected $E_{ij}$ are aggregated via Pearson's Chi-Square statistic with $\nu = (r-1)(c-1)$ degrees of freedom:

$$\chi^2 = \sum_{i=1}^r \sum_{j=1}^c \frac{(n_{ij} - E_{ij})^2}{E_{ij}} \xrightarrow[H_0]{d} \chi^2_\nu, \qquad p\text{-value} = P(\chi^2_\nu \ge \chi^2_{\text{obs}})$$

6. Theoretical Foundations: Statistical Independence vs. Causality Academic & Cybersecurity Report

1. Independence in Probability Theory vs. Finite-Sample Statistics

In axiomatic probability theory, two discrete random variables $X$ and $F$ defined on $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)$ are stochastically independent ($X \perp\!\!\!\perp F$) if and only if their joint probability mass function factorizes identically across the entire support: $$P(X = x_i, F = f_j) = P(X = x_i)\,P(F = f_j) \quad \forall (i, j)$$ Equivalently, knowing the realization of $X$ provides zero information about $F$: $P(F = f_j \mid X = x_i) = P(F = f_j)$ and the Shannon mutual information vanishes ($I(X; F) = 0$).

In empirical statistics, however, we only observe a finite sample of size $N$. Even when $H_0: X \perp\!\!\!\perp F$ is strictly true, random sampling fluctuations ensure that $n_{ij}/N \ne p_{i\bullet}p_{\bullet j}$. Pearson's $\chi^2$ test quantifies whether the normalized squared deviations $\sum_{i,j} (n_{ij} - E_{ij})^2 / E_{ij}$ exceed what can plausibly arise from multinomial sampling noise under the asymptotic $\chi^2_{(r-1)(c-1)}$ law.

2. Maximum Likelihood Expected Counts & Degrees of Freedom

Under the null hypothesis $H_0$, the unknown joint cell probabilities are parameterized by $\theta_{ij} = p_i q_j$ subject to $\sum_{i=1}^r p_i = 1$ and $\sum_{j=1}^c q_j = 1$. Maximizing the multinomial log-likelihood $\ell(p, q) = \sum_{i,j} n_{ij} \ln(p_i q_j)$ yields the Maximum Likelihood Estimators $\hat{p}_i = n_{i\bullet}/N$ and $\hat{q}_j = n_{\bullet j}/N$, which directly produce $E_{ij} = N\hat{p}_i\hat{q}_j = n_{i\bullet}n_{\bullet j}/N$.

Why are the degrees of freedom $\nu = (r - 1)(c - 1)$? An unconstrained $r \times c$ contingency table has $rc - 1$ free cell probabilities (since $\sum p_{ij} = 1$). Under $H_0$, we estimate $(r - 1)$ independent row marginals and $(c - 1)$ independent column marginals from the data. Subtracting estimated parameters gives: $$\nu = (rc - 1) - \big[(r - 1) + (c - 1)\big] = rc - r - c + 1 = (r - 1)(c - 1)$$

3. Statistical Association vs. Causality (Confounding & Simpson's Paradox)

Rejecting $H_0$ in a $\chi^2$ test establishes statistical association ($P(F \mid X) \ne P(F)$), which is a symmetric property of the joint distribution ($X \not\!\perp\!\!\!\perp F \iff F \not\!\perp\!\!\!\perp X$). Causality, by contrast, is an asymmetric structural property governed by interventional distributions: $X$ causes $F$ only if actively intervening on $X$ changes the distribution of $F$, i.e., $P(F \mid \text{do}(X = x_1)) \ne P(F \mid \text{do}(X = x_2))$.

  • Confounding (Common Cause $X \leftarrow Z \rightarrow F$): Two variables can exhibit a massive $\chi^2$ and $p < 10^{-10}$ with zero direct causal link if both are driven by a latent confounder $Z$.
  • Reverse Causality: Observational contingency tables cannot distinguish $X \to F$ from $F \to X$ without temporal ordering or randomized experiments.
  • Simpson's Paradox: Marginalizing over a confounder $Z$ can even invert the direction of association observed inside every conditional stratum $Z = z_k$.

4. Cybersecurity Implications: IDS Feature Selection & SOC Fallacies

In Cybersecurity and Network Telemetry, $\chi^2$ contingency analysis and Mutual Information $I(X; F) \approx \frac{G^2}{2N\ln 2}$ are foundational tools for automated feature selection in Intrusion Detection Systems (IDS). Before training a classifier, analysts test whether discrete packet attributes $X$ (e.g., protocol type, TCP flag bitmasks, destination port buckets) are statistically independent of malicious labels $F$. Features with negligible $\chi^2$ (high $p$-value) carry no discriminative signal and are pruned.

However, confusing statistical association with causality in a Security Operations Center (SOC) leads to brittle automated defenses: if a malware family happens to use a specific TLS cipher suite or cloud ASN ($X$) due to a confounder $Z$ (the attacker's default build template), blocking all traffic with attribute $X$ treats a correlational artifact as a causal root cause, causing severe false-positive outages on legitimate traffic.
